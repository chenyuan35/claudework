# From Locksmith to $1.2M ARR: The Solo Founder Who Stalled for Two Years Before Hiring

Tim Bennetto spent a decade as a locksmith in Melbourne. No degree, no coding background, no audience. Today his social media scheduling tool Pallyy clears $1.2M in annual revenue with a three-person team, 60,000 active users, and 1,500 paying customers.

The part nobody tells you? He plateaued at $1,300 MRR for nearly two years straight. That's not a growth story. That's a stall story that eventually compounded.

And the part that should make you uncomfortable? He almost quit. Not once. Multiple times. During those two flat years, Tim was watching his savings drain while the product showed zero signs of life. The only thing keeping him going was the same thing that keeps most founders going long past the point of rational decision-making: he'd already invested too much to walk away.

## $36K the First Year, Then Nothing Moved

Tim launched Pallyy (originally called ShareMyInsights) in 2019 after six months of learning HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Nuxt on Codecademy. The MVP took 30 days to build. His point of difference was a "share your Instagram analytics" feature.

Nobody wanted it.

The Product Hunt launch was uneventful. He had no audience, no marketing budget, no strategy. His first 100 customers came from a friend who handed over a free Instagram analytics tool for free and redirected its traffic to Tim's platform. At $5/month, that's $500 MRR. Better than zero. Not exactly hockey stick.

Then growth stopped. For almost two years.

"I had no audience," Tim wrote on Indie Hackers. "Launching was un-eventful." The sharing feature he'd built around? "Absolutely nobody wanted." He removed it and focused on the analytics dashboard instead.

Here's the thing that separates Tim from the 90% who quit: he didn't pivot away from the product. He pivoted the feature. There's a massive difference. Most founders, when their core feature flops, assume the entire product is dead and start over. Tim kept the analytics dashboard that people actually used and killed the one thing nobody wanted. That's surgical, not desperate.

He was burning through savings during this stretch. "Had some savings that I was going into, and got to $1K MRR relatively quick so that helped too," Tim recalled. "Was running out though, so luckily it started to grow!" That's the part of the story you don't see in the revenue charts. The $1K MRR wasn't a milestone to celebrate. It was a runway marker counting down.

What kept him going? Not vision. Not passion. Stubbornness and sunk cost. "If you don't keep going you're not giving yourself that opportunity," he wrote. That's not motivational poster material. That's a locksmith who'd already sunk four years into a thing and couldn't afford to start over.

## The API Change That Unlocked Everything

Instagram opened its API to allow anyone to publish posts. Within a week, Tim had built the scheduling feature. In the coming months he added other social platforms and it quickly became the #1 feature.

This wasn't a product pivot. It was a platform bet. When Instagram changed its rules, Tim was positioned to catch the wave. The scheduling feature turned ShareMyInsights into Pallyy. A $1,500 logo rebrand later, and the product had a new identity.

The revenue trajectory tells the story: $36,000 in 2021, $348,000 in 2022, $900,000 in 2023, $1.2 million in 2024. That's not a straight line. That's two flat years followed by a 10x.

Honestly, the two-year plateau is the story. Most founders would have declared product failure and moved on. Tim stayed. The question worth asking isn't "how did he grow?" It's "what did he do during the two years of nothing that set up the 10x?"

The answer is unglamorous. He kept shipping. He did at least four full redesigns of the product. "People were starting to get annoyed, but it got better each time," he admitted. He waited for Instagram to open its API. He positioned himself. That's not luck. That's patience with a thesis.

## The $2K Acquisition That Bought 2,000 Daily Visitors

Tim's most underrated move was buying other people's free tools.

He acquired free Instagram analytics tools for around $2,000 each. Each one came with 2,000+ daily visitors he redirected to Pallyy. That's not a growth hack. That's buying distribution that would have taken years to build organically.

"It was actually more than 2K visits per day," Tim noted. "However it's not easy to find tools that cheap."

Think about that math. A $2,000 acquisition bringing 60,000 monthly visitors. Compare that to Google Ads at $2-5 per click. The same traffic would cost $120,000+ monthly. Tim found a channel nobody was pricing correctly and bought it.

The SEO strategy compounded from there. He hired a content writer for blog posts and social posts at $50-100 per post. OpenAI costs ran $100-200 monthly. His marketing split shifted to 80% marketing, 20% coding after year two.

"Ideally if you could do 50/50 from day one that would be best," Tim admitted. He learned that lesson the hard way. Two years of code-only focus nearly killed the business. The 80/20 marketing-heavy split that followed year two is what produced the 10x growth.

The blog content strategy was relentless. Tim wrote 80+ blog posts on similar topics. Not because each one was brilliant. Because SEO is a numbers game at the start, and volume compounds. Each post targeted a long-tail keyword. Each post aged. Each post linked to Pallyy's tools. The compounding effect didn't show up in month one. It showed up in year three.

## 40% Commission, Lifetime: The Affiliate Bet

Tim's most unconventional decision was giving away 40% of revenue to affiliates. Not 10%, not 15%. Forty percent. For the lifetime of the customer.

"Some people say 40% is too much to giveaway," Tim wrote. "But I'd rather 60% of a sale that I'd never of seen, so it works for me."

The math is brutal at first glance. On a $29/month plan, an affiliate earns $11.60 every month the customer stays. If the customer stays 12 months, the affiliate pockets $139.20. Tim keeps $208.80. Over 24 months, it's $278.40 vs $417.60.

Here's what most founders miss: affiliates bring backlinks. Every affiliate who writes about Pallyy creates a backlink that helps Pallyy's other pages rank. The 22% of MRR from affiliates is the floor, not the ceiling. Tim estimates the SEO value alone makes the program worth it.

He manages the whole thing with Rewardful, spending about 10 minutes per month on affiliate payments. The program runs on autopilot.

The goal now? $1 million in revenue earned through affiliates. That's not a side channel. That's a business within a business.

Here's what's wild about the affiliate math. Tim pays 40% lifetime. That means if a customer stays for 3 years on a $29 plan, the affiliate earns $417.60 total. Tim keeps $626.40. Most SaaS founders would call that giving away the company. Tim calls it buying customers he'd never have reached.

The logic holds up. Tim's product is a social media scheduling tool. His affiliates are social media creators and agencies. They're not just reselling. They're recommending something they'd use anyway, to an audience that trusts them. The 40% isn't a commission. It's an acquisition cost that's only paid when the acquisition succeeds. Try getting that deal from Facebook Ads.

## The Solo-to-Team Transition

For four years, Tim did everything himself. Code, design, support, marketing, SEO. Every single function.

His first hire was a senior developer. Then he planned customer support and marketing roles. The team now sits at three people.

"It's been amazing doing everything by myself for 4 years, but it's time to expand," Tim wrote. "It's just allowed me to start to expand the team, other than that nothing really."

That last sentence is the realest thing in this whole story. A $74K MRR solo business didn't change Tim's lifestyle. It just let him hire. The myth that revenue equals freedom is a lie. Revenue equals the ability to build a team. That's it. The freedom comes later, if the team works.

Tim's mistake? He focused solely on product for the first two years. "My biggest mistake was focusing solely on product," he told Indie Hackers. The marketing came later. Way later. Two years later.

The team expansion wasn't a victory lap. It was a survival move. At $74K MRR solo, Tim was the bottleneck for every support ticket, every bug fix, every SEO decision, every feature request. The business couldn't grow past his personal capacity. The first hire, a senior developer, freed Tim to focus on growth. That's the trade: give away the code you love writing, get back the growth you've been neglecting.

Three people. $1.2M ARR. That's $400K revenue per employee. Compare that to Hootsuite's ~$200K per employee, or Buffer's ~$150K. Tim's team efficiency is 2-3x industry benchmarks. That's not because he's a genius manager. It's because he stayed solo too long and learned to do everything himself before delegating.

## The Playbook: What to Actually Steal

**1. Buy distribution, don't build it.** Find free tools in your space that owners want to offload. $2,000 for 2,000 daily visitors is the cheapest acquisition channel nobody's pricing correctly.

**2. Give affiliates lifetime commissions at 40%.** Yes, it sounds like too much. But you're paying for sales you'd never have seen. Plus the backlinks compound your SEO. The floor is the direct revenue. The ceiling is the organic ranking lift.

**3. When the platform changes its API, be first.** Instagram opened post publishing. Tim had scheduling built within a week. The window for being first is measured in days, not months.

**4. Two years of plateau is not failure.** It's the cost of compounding. Tim's two flat years preceded a 10x. The question isn't whether to quit. It's whether you're compounding during the flat period.

**5. Solo to team is a financial decision, not a lifestyle upgrade.** Revenue doesn't buy freedom. It buys the ability to hire. The freedom comes when the team runs without you. Tim's at step one.

## The Question That Stays

Tim Bennetto spent 10 years as a locksmith. He built a $1.2M ARR SaaS with three people. He gave away 40% of revenue to affiliates. He bought free tools for distribution. He waited two years for an API change that turned his business around.

The question isn't whether you could do the same thing. The question is whether you'd have stayed during the two-year plateau. When the savings are draining and the MRR chart is a flat line, the rational move is to quit. Tim didn't. That's not because he was brave. It's because he was a locksmith who'd already sunk four years into a thing and couldn't afford to start over.

Most founders in that position fold. The ones who don't aren't necessarily smarter. They're just more stubborn. And sometimes, stubbornness compounds into $1.2M.
