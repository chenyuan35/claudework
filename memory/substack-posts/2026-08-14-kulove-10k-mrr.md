# Coding by Hand is Dead. He Let AI Do Everything — $10K MRR in 6 Months

The day Kulove got laid off, he hadn't written a line of code in a year. Not one. AI had written all of it — roughly 7,000 GitHub commits, every single one "valid," he insists. He was let go anyway. Part of a quota, he says. He fought for his job, lobbied, negotiated. Lost.

So on January 30, 2026, he went full-time indie. Six months later he woke up and checked his dashboard: MRR just crossed $10K. That's four times where he started. He shared the numbers on V2EX, China's most honest developer forum — the place where people roast you for existing.

The replies were brutal. "One year, zero lines of code written, all AI — no wonder you got laid off." That's the top comment. But Kulove didn't hide the AI usage. He led with it.

This is his story. The part that matters isn't the AI. It's what he did differently around it.

## The Year of Zero Handwritten Code

Kulove is a senior engineer. He says he held senior-level ratings at several companies across his career. Then came the layoff year — the year he wrote nothing by hand.

"I didn't write a single line of code all year, everything was done by AI," he posted. "So I started having endless anxiety."

The anxiety wasn't about being replaced. It was about the future of his whole career track. His reasoning: AI will first automate the work of programmers. If you're a developer whose value is writing code, your value is being eroded in real time. He decided to exit the employee path before the exit was forced on him twice.

He was ahead of the curve on this — he saw it coming. The layoff just made it official.

His workflow: Claude Code, with heavy prompt discipline. He splits requirements into small pieces, watches the AI's context length per feature, reviews the frontend result, tests features, and rejects and retries prompts until output is right. "Most people who say AI is bad just don't know how to use it," he told the forum.

One detail stands out. He described how he works with AI: run the task, check the frontend result first, test the feature, skim the code if it feels off, and if it's bad, reject and re-prompt. "Claude Code works much better than other tools," he wrote. "Most people who say AI is bad just don't know how to use it." Prompt discipline, not the model, is the moat.

The pace was brutal while he was still employed. Nearly 7,000 commits that year, "all valid," he stressed — and he went to bed at 2 or 3 a.m. most nights. When a commenter mocked the commit count as AI spam, Kulove's reply was patient: define your standards, review what the AI produces, and commit code you'd be proud to sign. He wasn't automating sloppiness. He was automating a workflow he'd spent a decade building by hand.

## $2,500 to $10K in Six Months

When he announced going full-time, he was transparent about the starting point: MRR around $2,500, net profit around $2,000 after costs. "Far less than my salary," he admitted. But growing.

By July 31 — exactly six months after quitting — he posted an update. "Income is higher now, lucky, growth has been good every month. Woke up today and MRR just crossed 10K."

$2,500 → $10K+ MRR in six months. A 4x in two quarters, solo, no funding, no team.

He's anonymous about the product: an Agent-related SaaS for overseas users, no Chinese version, China IPs blocked. "Product and income — I can only share one," he joked. V2EX users understood. The audience that roasts him is the same audience that would grind his free tier to dust.

The product itself came from a personal pain point. He went to Reddit — the AI subreddits, specifically — and checked whether people were complaining about the same thing. Existing tools were bad. "If there's demand and the current tools are bad, attack from the UX + AI angle." He never chases trending keywords, he said. No domain squatting on the next model release. No 48-hour launches. He called those "meaningless websites."

## The Failure the Forum Won't Let Him Forget

This story has two failures, and one of them is ongoing.

Failure one: getting laid off at all. He's honest that it stung. He calls his severance "receiving the god of layoffs" — the darkly funny Chinese internet term for a generous severance package. N+3, in his case. It bought him runway. Without it, he says, he might not have dared.

Failure two: Chinese users. He tried domestic products for years — mini-programs, apps, "never any traction." The breakthrough only came when he went overseas. His explanation is blunt: "Simplified-Chinese users freeload too much. They really try to milk you dry."

The domestic years are easy to skip past, and they're the most important part. Before the overseas product, he had built mini-programs and apps for the Chinese market. Nothing. Years of silence. "It wasn't until I made overseas products that I got some positive feedback," he wrote. The product that finally took off started in March 2025. The visible $2.5K-to-$10K curve sits on top of a decade of invisible failure — which is exactly why the forum's "you're just lucky" takes are so wrong.

He has receipts. One product offered a free tier with 500K tokens/month per user plus a referral bonus of another 500K. A roleplay group — possibly a "charity site," he speculates, but the database was a million rounds of roleplay dialogue — reverse-engineered the system and farmed it at scale. He lost a few hundred dollars in two or three days. Cloudflare shields, IP limits, device fingerprints. "There's always a way around them," he shrugged.

The forum attacked him for that too. "If you're so afraid of Chinese users freeloading, why post here at all?" He answered calmly: the product is overseas-only, no Chinese version, no China traffic. He posts to share experience, not to promote.

Then he added the line that made me laugh: "You think I'm bragging? I'm a scrub, fine. Is this forum owned by your family? Can't I post?"

Zero regrets. Pathetic, but true.

## His Playbook: Four Things He Did Differently

Here's what nobody talks about. His playbook isn't "use AI to ship 50 products." It's the opposite.

**Step 1: Pick a pain point, verify on Reddit, ignore the hot words.** His product came from his own frustration. He went to Reddit (the AI subreddits) and checked: is there demand? Do existing products suck? "If there's demand and the current tools are bad, attack from the UX + AI angle." He explicitly refuses to chase trending keywords — the "every new model = domain squatting + ship in 48 hours" play. "Those sites mean nothing to me."

**Step 2: Spend 3-6 months per product.** Most indie hackers spray ten products a year and pray. He spends a quarter to half a year on one. "Two-thirds of my time goes to improving user experience." User feedback? All positive, he says. His commits aren't feature spam — 7,000 commits of polish.

**Step 3: Set up the boring infrastructure.** US LLC via Stripe Atlas (Delaware, around $575), US bank card, Stripe for payments. "The US bank card payment flow is really stable." He recommends this to anyone going overseas. It's unsexy. It works.

**Step 4: Distribution the contrarian way.** He tested Reddit posts and Product Hunt launches. "Effect is not good, honestly." What works: SEO — "overseas is different from China, people still use websites" — plus ads. In a world where everyone screams "Reddit is the growth hack," the quiet guy says SEO, of all things.

**Step 5: Defend the margins.** Free tier abuse is a real cost line. He cuts trial quotas, requires card binding, blocks entire countries. His default stance: overseas customers only. The US market pays. The Chinese market, for his product class, steals.

Look at that list again. Nothing in it is clever. Reddit verification, slow builds, LLC paperwork, SEO, quota limits — every step is the boring version of what the indie world posts screenshots about. That's the point. He optimized for survival, not virality. Virality is a byproduct, not a strategy — and it's the first thing to break when the algorithm changes.

## The Part That's Uncomfortable to Admit

Six months in, he posted about his life. Time is freer: wakes up naturally, sometimes doesn't open a computer all week. Visited three countries, a week each. Got fatter. Has no new friends. "My ability to express myself has seriously declined — understandable after being alone for half a year."

One commenter replied with a line that deserves framing: "I can only tell people it's going well. Sometimes you can't even tell your family." Kulove agreed without drama. The $10K MRR is public. The loneliness is private. That gap is the real indie tax, and nobody charges it to your card.

And then the line that stopped me: "I'm more lost than before. I used to envy digital nomads. Now that I actually am one, I found out this path is also pretty boring."

He's earning $10K/month and he's lonely and bored and confused. Nobody puts that in the tweet.

But he's also not going back. "I do think about finding a job sometimes. But the environment is bad now, and I don't want to grind anymore."

## What This Actually Means

Kulove's bet was that AI would first eat the "write code" job. He bet on it with his career — then used the tool to build the thing that replaced his old job. He's not a great coder anymore. He's a great reviewer. He reads AI output, tests it, rejects it, and re-prompts until it's right. The scarce skill shifted from "producing code" to "judging code."

He's also a living counterexample to the content-farm indie scene. No 48-hour launches. No Product Hunt rituals. No hype threads. One product, six months, $10K MRR, and a forum thread full of people calling him a fraud.

The funniest part? In that thread, another dev wrote: "Honestly, $2,000 MRR is already above 98% of Chinese indie developers. Most people can't even get to $200." And Kulove just replied: "Yeah, it's really hard. It took me years to figure out the patterns."

Years. Not months. He'd been building domestic products for years before the overseas product finally worked — the one that took off was started in March 2025. The visible six months of $2.5K → $10K sits on top of invisible years of nothing.

So when you see the screenshot of $10K MRR, remember what's not in it: the failed mini-programs, the free-tier farmers who cost him hundreds of dollars, the forum strangers telling him he deserved to be fired, and the six months of waking up alone in Chiang Mai with no one to talk to.

He'd tell you the AI did the coding. He'd also tell you the hard part was never the code.
