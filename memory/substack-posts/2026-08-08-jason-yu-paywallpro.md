# $120K With No Code: How a Chinese PM Ran a 20-App Empire on 50/50 Splits

Subtitle: He never wrote a line of code. He still out-earned his salary 6 to 1 in month one — by hiring mercenary developers on revenue splits and letting 18 dead apps wait for their moment.

---

It was 2 a.m. in Xi'an, and Jason Yu was testing OPPO phones like a factory worker. His employer, a big Chinese company doing overseas apps, needed every screen verified on every Android model — OPPO, vivo, Huawei, a desk full of test devices that turned his nights into an assembly line. He'd at last crawled into bed when his boss called at six to fix a bug. He looked out the window at the grey city and thought: I'm a donkey. A donkey pulling a mill.

That same month, his secret side project earned $10,000.

Not bad for an app he'd built in 15 days with a developer friend. No marketing. No launch. No Twitter thread. Only natural traffic. He woke up, checked the account, and there it was: a few thousand yuan richer. His full-time salary that month? ¥12,000. Roughly $1,700.

The math was so stupid it made him laugh. Six months of donkey work, erased by one correct product cut.

Here's the part that makes this story different from every other indie hacker case I've written: Jason never learned to code. Not one line. He's a product manager who ran a 20-app empire by hiring mercenary developers on profit splits and out-screening everyone else's risk. His flagship product today — PaywallPro, a paywall intelligence SaaS used by app teams to study 46,000+ iOS paywalls — grew out of a habit of downloading 500 apps and staring at their paywalls like a weirdo.

Total haul across three years: roughly $120K. One app did most of the work.

## The 15-Day App That Paid More Than His Job

The first product was almost embarrassingly simple: add text to photos. Fifteen days from idea to App Store. The developer was a colleague at the same company. Split: 50/50, no salary, pure revenue share.

It made $300-400 a day. Every day. From nothing.

Nobody pushed it. No TikTok account, no launch post, no "build in public" thread. The app store was doing the selling, and the paywall did the asking. Jason's only job was picking what to build next and finding someone who could build it faster than his employer could fire him.

That month's lesson stuck: making money didn't need a grand strategy. It needed a correct product opening. One right cut.

He didn't quit his job. Not yet. That's another thing worth noting — the first product's success didn't trigger a dramatic resignation. It triggered a parallel life. Days at the company, nights running an invisible app business. The donkey kept pulling the mill, but now the mill was his.

## The Mercenary Playbook: No Salary, Only Splits

Every non-technical founder hits the same wall: I can't code, so who builds this?

Jason's answer was hiring like a warlord, not like an HR department. No base pay. Revenue split from day one. First product: 50/50. The deal was simple — you build, I find the market, we both eat.

The betrayals came fast. One guy took the designs and vanished. Another quit halfway to go back to his job. One literally forked the code and shipped his own version of the product.

Jason didn't get angry. He got systematic. In the lawless early phase, screening was the only risk control. If you want to steal my design and my idea, go ahead — because code isn't the moat here. The brain that smells what's about to be hot? That's the moat.

His screening trick was gloriously untechnical: he looked at pixels.

Couldn't read the code quality, so he judged UI fidelity. A developer who nails rounded corners, shadows, and spacing pixel-perfect? His logic is probably solid too. That precision signals something rare — the guy cares about details. Plenty of strong coders exist. Few are willing to die on the details with you. Those are the ones who last.

The screening worked. As his hit rate climbed, the balance of power flipped. Splits went from 50/50 to 80/20. Nobody complained. Everyone knew the truth by then: coders are everywhere. People who can lead you to money are not.

## The 10% Law: 20 Apps, 2 Winners

Here's what most people get wrong about indie development. They imagine one product, polished for three years, a masterpiece that changes the world.

Jason laughed at that. He made 20 apps and 12 websites in three years.

Two or three of them made real money.

That's the brutal 10% law. Build one product and your failure rate is 90%. Build ten, and your odds of survival go way up. Early on, quantity beats quality. Ship fast, ship ugly, validate. Most people can't stomach this because it feels like wasting effort. But each failure is tuition for the next pick.

What did the 18 failures look like? The usual indie graveyard: a drawing app nobody found, a utility buried by a bigger competitor, a game with decent reviews and zero distribution. Jason tracked them like inventory, not like children. Each one taught him something about the market's appetite — and a few taught him what to never build again.

## The Zombie App That Came Back to Life

Here's where Jason's story gets weird and specific.

Most developers see a product with no traffic and kill it. Shut down the server, pull the listing, cut losses. Jason did the opposite. If server costs were manageable, he let his dead apps rot in the store like zombies. Not because he was lazy. Because products die, but trends don't.

He had a pixel-avatar app. Long-term half-dead: a dozen downloads a day, maybe $100 a month. Any sane founder would've deleted it.

Then, one year, Ghibli-style filters exploded across social media.

Jason smelled it. He grabbed the half-dead app and added a Ghibli filter. One month later, revenue went from $200 to $4,000 a month. Twenty times. From a corpse.

That's the thing nobody tells you about a portfolio of "failed" products: every app is an unopened lottery ticket. You only have to keep it on the table. Don't leave the game.

The economics made it easy to stay patient. An abandoned app with no traffic costs almost nothing — a few dollars a month in hosting, a renewal fee, an hour of your attention per quarter. Compared to the upside of one lucky trend hit, that's free optionality. Jason treated the dead apps the way a farmer treats fallow land: resting, not worthless.

## He Studies Paywalls Like a Psychopath

After dozens of products, Jason developed an occupational disease.

His phone carried 500 apps at all times. He downloaded apps and stared at their paywalls — the screens that ask for money — with a level of attention most people reserve for their ex's social media. He dissected how they asked, when they asked, what they promised, which words appeared next to the price.

His finding: 90% of subscription revenue comes from the first open. The onboarding moment. If you don't ask well right there, you're leaving 90% of your money on the table.

Many Chinese developers felt embarrassed asking for money up front. They buried the paywall deep in settings, like it was shameful. In the overseas market, that's suicide. Western users are trained: good products cost money. Ask early, ask clearly, ask confidently.

He turned that research into his current flagship — PaywallPro, a tool that helps app teams study how top apps structure paywalls, onboarding, and pricing. It started as his private research project and grew into a business with a database of 46,000+ iOS paywalls. G2 lists it as subscription intelligence for app developers. In May 2026, he opened the top 500 paywalls to the public as a free dataset, and the company page now points at a repo expanding toward 4,000+ subscription apps with paywalls, onboarding flows, and pricing history.

From making apps to selling the method of making money from apps. It's the classic arbitrage: he did the pattern-matching, then sold the pattern.

The transition wasn't glamorous. It started as a designer-turned-product founder side quest — a research project he ran while his apps kept ticking over. Then he noticed something: the teams who asked him for paywall advice kept coming back. They had the same blind spot he'd once had. They'd polish features for months, then throw a random pricing screen at the user and wonder why nobody converted. PaywallPro packaged his 500-app obsession into a repeatable service. No consulting hours, no workshops. A product.

## What He Actually Did Differently

Let me line up the moves, because they're copyable even if you write code all day:

**Split the upside, not the salary.** No base pay, pure revenue share. It filters for confidence — a developer who won't take 50/50 of zero doesn't believe in the product. It also means your burn rate is zero.

**Judge people by pixels.** Can't evaluate code? Evaluate craft. UI fidelity signals character. Care about the details = probably care about the business.

**Ship 10 to survive 1.** The 10% law is real. Diversify your shots. One polished failure is worse than ten ugly experiments.

**Keep dead products breathing.** If it costs $5 a month to host, leave it. Trends come back. The Ghibli moment made $3,800 extra on an app everyone else would've killed.

**Ask for money like it's your job.** Because it is. 90% of subscription revenue is decided in the first open. Your paywall is not a settings page detail; it's the product's front door.

**Sell the shovel, not the gold.** His real business today isn't an app — it's teaching other teams how to monetize theirs. The most profitable move in a gold rush is always the pick-and-shovel play.

## One Last Thing About the Donkey

The image that stuck with me: Jason at 2 a.m. in Xi'an, testing OPPO phones, being called at 6 a.m. to fix a bug. And the same month, a 15-day app making $10,000 while he slept.

He says he's still testing. Still hunting bugs. The difference? Now he tests human nature and hunts for bugs in the market.

That's the trade, and I think about it whenever I hear someone say they're waiting for the right time to start. Jason didn't wait for skills. He waited for nothing. He made the app in 15 days, split the money 50/50, and let a dead app sit in the store until the world's taste caught up with his inventory.

He never wrote a line of code. He simply paid attention to what people would pay for, and he never left the table.

So — what's sitting in your store right now, waiting for its Ghibli moment?
